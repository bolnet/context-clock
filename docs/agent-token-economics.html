05The clock

The timer starts when you send

A cache entry lives five minutes. The subtlety is when those minutes begin — at the start of the request, not when the response finishes streaming.

send ├──────────────────────────────────────────────┤ entry expires │ ├─ 3 min generating ─┐ └─ two minutes left to send the next one
  • Generation time is spent inside the window. A three-minute response leaves two minutes of thinking time, not five.
  • A read refreshes the timer, free. Continuous traffic keeps a prefix alive indefinitely — a busy session is a cheap session.
  • So size is not the enemy. Silence is. You can carry 300,000 tokens all day for pennies a turn, provided you keep touching it.

10Scaling

A pause is a tax, not a toll

12Unplanned finding

A cache can expire while you are working

Four misses arrived inside the five-minute window — one after a gap of just ten seconds — in sessions where nobody was idle at all.

16Limits

What this does not say

  • The paused session is a worst case. It forces expiry at every turn boundary; real work does not. Uninterrupted is the floor, this is the ceiling — a working day lands between, and where depends on habits rather than context size.
  • The two arms ran to different lengths — 14 turns against 16 — because each stopped on its own judgement. The total-cost gap is therefore not attributable to the clock alone. The miss count, hit rate, bucket shares and per-miss curve are length-independent; those are the quotable results.
  • One model, one task family, one provider. The 12.5× ratio and bucket structure are general. The specific shares are not.
  • Session totals vary ±58% on identical inputs. Rates and shares reproduce to the digit.
  • One known instrument flaw. A request's elapsed time is measured from its first attempt, so a retried request under-reports the true gap. Recorded rather than silently corrected.
